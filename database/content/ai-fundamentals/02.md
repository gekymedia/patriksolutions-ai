# Module 2 — What AI Can and Cannot Do

**Estimated time:** ~55 minutes · **Lessons:** 4

## Learning objectives
By the end of this module you will be able to:
- List the tasks generative AI is genuinely great at.
- Recognize the tasks where it struggles or fails.
- Explain what an AI "hallucination" is and why it happens.
- Decide, before you start, whether a task is a good fit for AI — and how much to trust the answer.

---

## Lesson 2.1 — The superpowers (what AI is genuinely great at)

Generative AI shines when a task involves **language, patterns, drafts, or transformation**. Its real strengths:

- **Writing & rewriting** — emails, messages, posts, summaries, outlines, first drafts of almost anything.
- **Explaining & teaching** — breaking a hard topic into simple words, at any level you ask for.
- **Summarizing** — turning a long article, transcript, or document into the key points.
- **Changing the form of text** — turning notes into a letter, a letter into bullet points, formal into friendly, English into Spanish.
- **Brainstorming** — 20 name ideas, 10 angles for a post, pros and cons of a decision.
- **Organizing messy information** — turning a jumble of thoughts into a clean table or checklist.
- **Answering general "how do I…" questions** — a patient tutor that never gets tired.

The pattern: AI is a phenomenal **first-draft machine and thinking partner**. It gets you from a blank page to something you can react to and improve — fast. That "blank page to draft" leap is where most of the time savings in this whole course comes from.

---

## Lesson 2.2 — The weak spots (where AI struggles or fails)

Knowing the limits is what separates confident users from embarrassed ones. AI tends to struggle with:

- **Exact facts, numbers, dates, names, and quotes.** It may produce a real-*looking* statistic, citation, or quote that is simply made up. Never trust these without checking.
- **Very recent events.** A model's training has a cutoff date. Unless the tool has live web access turned on, it may not know about anything recent — and may not tell you it doesn't know.
- **Math and precise calculations.** It can slip on arithmetic because it's predicting words, not truly "computing." (Some tools now use a built-in calculator for this — helpful, but verify anything important.)
- **Counting and strict rules.** "Give me exactly 7 words" or "don't use the letter e" can trip it up.
- **Your private or internal information.** It doesn't know your company's numbers, your customer's order, or today's weather unless you provide it.
- **Deep, novel reasoning with high stakes.** It's a great assistant for legal, medical, and financial *understanding*, but it is **not** a lawyer, doctor, or accountant. For decisions that affect your health, money, or legal standing, use it to get oriented, then confirm with a qualified human.

A simple filter: **the more a task depends on a single exact fact being correct, the more you must verify it.** The more it's about language, structure, or ideas, the more you can lean on AI.

---

## Lesson 2.3 — "Hallucinations": the thing everyone needs to understand

This is the single most important concept for using AI safely.

A **hallucination** is when AI produces information that sounds confident and plausible but is **false or invented** — a fake statistic, a book that doesn't exist, a wrong date stated as certain, a made-up quote attributed to a real person.

**Why it happens** (straight from Module 1): the model predicts *plausible next words*. If a convincing-sounding but false sentence fits the pattern, it may produce it. It has no built-in "do I actually know this is true?" check. It isn't lying — lying needs intent. It's confidently guessing.

**What it looks like in real life:**
- You ask for sources, and it gives you three article titles with authors and dates — two of which don't exist.
- You ask about a product's specs and it states a number that's wrong.
- You ask "what's the population of my town?" and it gives a confident figure that's years out of date or invented.

**Your defense is simple and it's a habit, not a tool:**
1. **Assume facts need checking.** Treat AI output as a confident draft, not a verified source.
2. **Verify anything that matters** — names, numbers, dates, quotes, sources, anything you'll act on, send, or publish — with a quick search or a trusted source.
3. **Ask the AI to help you check:** "What would I search to verify this?" or "How confident are you, and what might be wrong here?" It will often flag its own shaky spots.

Hallucinations aren't a reason to avoid AI — they're a reason to *use it like a smart intern's draft*: hugely useful, never the final word on facts.

## Lesson 2.4 — The "good fit?" decision in 10 seconds

Before you hand a task to AI, run it through three quick questions:

1. **Is it mostly about language, ideas, drafting, or reshaping text?** → Great fit. Lean in.
2. **Does success depend on exact facts, fresh data, or precise math?** → Use AI to draft, then **verify the hard parts** yourself.
3. **Would being wrong hurt someone's health, money, safety, or legal standing?** → Use AI to understand and prepare, but **get a qualified human** to confirm the decision.

Most everyday tasks land in #1 and #2 — which is exactly where you'll spend this course.

---

## 🎬 Instructor demonstration script

1. **Show a strength.** Prompt: `Summarize the following in 5 bullet points, then rewrite it as a friendly text message:` and paste a few messy paragraphs of notes. Point out how fast "messy → clean → friendly" happened.
2. **Trigger a likely hallucination (safely).** Prompt: `Give me three academic studies, with authors and years, that prove [a very niche or recent claim].` When it produces confident-looking citations, say: *"These look real. Some probably aren't. This is a hallucination — never cite these without checking each one exists."*
3. **Show the self-check trick.** Prompt: `For each of those studies, how could I verify it's real, and how confident are you it exists?` Watch it hedge. Say: *"Asking it to grade its own confidence is a free safety net."*
4. **Show a math slip (maybe).** Prompt: `What is 4,892 × 357? Show your steps.` Verify on a calculator. If it's right, great; if it's off, perfect teaching moment: *"Always double-check numbers that matter."*
5. Recap the three "good fit?" questions on screen.

## 🛠️ Hands-on exercise (15 min)
1. Pick something factual about your own town or field (a population, a rule, a price, a date).
2. Ask the AI for it **without** giving it any source. Write down the answer.
3. Now verify it with one quick web search or a trusted source. Was the AI right, outdated, or invented?
4. Ask the AI: `What should I have been careful about when trusting your last answer?`
5. Write one sentence: "I will always double-check AI when ______."

**You're done when** you've personally caught either a correct answer you *could* have been burned by, or an actual mistake. Either outcome teaches the habit.

## ✅ Quiz (with answers & explanations)

**Q1. Which task is the *best* fit for generative AI?**
A) Confirming today's exact stock price
B) Rewriting a rude email to sound polite
C) Deciding your medication dosage
D) Giving you a guaranteed-correct legal verdict
**Answer: B.** Rewriting/reshaping language is a core strength. A, C, and D need live data or a qualified human.

**Q2. An AI "hallucination" is…**
A) A visual glitch on screen
B) Confident output that is actually false or invented
C) When the AI is slow
D) A feature you turn on
**Answer: B.** It's plausible-sounding but untrue information, produced confidently.

**Q3. Why do hallucinations happen?**
A) The AI is angry
B) It predicts plausible words and has no built-in truth check
C) Bad internet
D) It ran out of memory
**Answer: B.** Word-prediction optimizes for *plausible*, not *true*.

**Q4. You asked AI for three sources and it gave titles, authors, and dates. What should you do?**
A) Cite them immediately — they're detailed
B) Check that each source actually exists before using it
C) Assume at least one is fake and never use AI again
D) Email the authors
**Answer: B.** Detailed ≠ real. Verify each one exists.

**Q5. True or false: If a task's success depends on one exact number being correct, you should verify that number even if the AI sounds sure.**
**Answer: True.** Confidence is not accuracy. Verify facts that matter.

## 📋 Checklist — "Before I trust this AI answer"
- [ ] Is this mostly language/ideas (safe to lean on) or exact facts (must verify)?
- [ ] Did I check any names, numbers, dates, quotes, or sources?
- [ ] Could the info be out of date? Did I confirm it's current if that matters?
- [ ] Would being wrong hurt health, money, safety, or legal standing? If yes → confirm with a human.
- [ ] Did I ask the AI where it might be wrong?

## Summary & next steps
AI is a superb drafting and thinking partner for anything language-shaped, and unreliable for exact facts, fresh data, and precise math. Hallucinations are confident-but-false outputs caused by word-prediction — and your simple habit of "assume facts need checking, verify what matters" keeps you safe. You now know *what* to hand AI and how much to trust it.
